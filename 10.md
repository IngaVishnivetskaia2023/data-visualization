## Data Ink Ratio (after Edward Tufte)

The Data Ink Ratio is a concept introduced by Edward Tufte in his book "The Visual Display of Quantitative Information." 
It is a measure of **how much of a visualization is made up of actual data versus non-essential elements** that do not convey information. 

Here’s a breakdown:

- **Understanding Data Ink:** Data ink refers to the actual ink (or, in modern terms, pixels) that is used to represent the data in a graphic. This includes the graphical elements like **bars, lines, and data labels**.

- **Total Ink Usage:** Total ink usage encompasses **all elements used in the graphic**, including decorative aspects like backgrounds, grid lines, shadows, and other non-data ink components.

- **Calculating the Ratio:** The Data Ink Ratio can be calculated using the formula:
```
Data Ink Ratio = Data Ink / Total Ink Used
```
A higher ratio indicates that more of the graphic is dedicated to conveying data rather than decorative elements.

#### Purpose and Goal: 
The goal is to maximize this ratio. A chart with a **high Data Ink Ratio** is more **effective** as it allows the viewer to focus on the data rather than being distracted by unnecessary elements.

**Practical Example:** For instance, if a chart has excessive background color, thick gridlines, or shadows, these elements may distract viewers from interpreting the data accurately. By removing or simplifying these non-essential elements, you increase the clarity of the data representation.

In summary, maintaining a high Data Ink Ratio is crucial for effective data visualization, making it easier for audiences to understand and interpret the information presented.
